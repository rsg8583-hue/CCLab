How to interact

  1. Sweep up plastic Press and drag across the water. Every piece you pass over joins the pile and follows you until you let go.
  2. Drop it on a zone Recycle takes the pile out of the water. Convert swaps it for paper, which then fades away.
  3. Slow the flow New plastic drifts in every simulated year. Each piece you convert slows it, and after 60 conversions it stops, so the water can finally clear.

About the simulation

The real problem

Each year an estimated 11 to 12 million metric tons of plastic waste enter the ocean. Most of it comes from land, carried by rivers, and breaks into ever smaller fragments that pollute the surface and the seabed and harm marine life. One widely cited study put the number of pieces already floating at the surface above 5 trillion.

A conceptual model, not a forecast

The plastic counter starts at that estimate of 5.25 trillion pieces, at about 175 billion pieces per object, and one simulated year passes every second at normal speed. After the starting point, the rate of growth is chosen for the artwork and does not predict real pollution levels.

Conversion is a metaphor

Converting stands for choosing alternatives before plastic is made, which is why it slows new plastic and recycling does not. It is not a way to clean up the sea. Paper still takes resources to make, can have plastic coatings, and does not dissolve harmlessly the moment it reaches the water.

Why the water has a limit

To keep the page responsive, the water shows at most 200 pieces at once. Past that, new plastic is still added to the counter but is not drawn. The limit is about rendering, not a sign that the real problem levels off.

Figures: Pew Charitable Trusts & SYSTEMIQ, Breaking the Plastic Wave (2020); Eriksen et al., PLOS ONE (2014).
